The long read · Since 1998

How a red-sauce room on Mulberry Street turned into five restaurants and one very long reservation book

Sal and Teresa Valentine only wanted to stop turning people away. Twenty-eight years later their daughter runs five kitchens — and still answers the phone on Saturdays.

By Dolores Kaminski · Photographs by Theo Park · October 12, 2026 · 9 min read

The first night, October 1998, there were eleven tables, one burner that worked properly and a line out the door by seven. Sal Valentine had cooked on cruise ships and in other men's kitchens for twenty years; Teresa kept the book in a spiral notebook by the phone. By the end of the first winter the notebook had a waiting list, and the waiting list had its own notebook.

"We weren't trying to be famous," Teresa says now, from the same banquette by the window. "We were trying to feed the people who were already standing on the sidewalk." The gravy — pork shoulder, braciole, sausage, six hours — went on at ten every morning. It still does.

Their daughter Rosa grew up in the back booth doing homework between the bread basket and the cash drawer. In 2006 she took over the lease on a shuttered luncheonette on Ludlow Street, kept the counter and the stools, and put a griddle where the soda fountain had been. Ludlow Counter has never taken a reservation, and never will.

The oysters came next. Astor Oyster Room opened on Lafayette in 2011, in a former printing shop with the type cases still bolted to the wall. Then the chophouse on Grand Street, in 2017, with a wood fire and a zinc bar long enough to have its own weather.

We never meant to open five restaurants. We meant to stop turning people away on Mulberry Street.Rosa Valentine, owner

The last to open, in 2023, was the one Rosa says her father would have liked best: a supper club on Seventh Avenue South with a quartet on a riser, a mezzanine with a curtain and a kitchen that sends out veal chops until one in the morning.

Five restaurants means five rooms, five kitchens and five chefs who do not always agree. What holds them together is the book: one reservation system, one phone line answered from Mulberry Street, one record of who likes the corner table and who is allergic to shellfish. "My mother's notebook," Rosa says, "just with better handwriting."

On a Monday in October the flagship is full by twenty to seven. Chef Tomás Ibarra has already struck the veal parmigiana off the board. At the host stand, Marco Bellini is quoting the bar for half past nine, and nobody is leaving.
